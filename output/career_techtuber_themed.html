Joining Teams

Starting in 2021, Chao joined the Teams organization, continuing to work on the vision pipeline and collaborating with the Teams CMD team on real product features, keeping his career tightly aligned with AI as it shifted toward large language models and multimodal systems.

Traditional AI: linear pipelines and fixed roles

In traditional AI products, work followed a linear path—data collection, model structure, training, then deployment—with data engineers, model teams, developers, and product each owning their own narrow slice.

That setup worked but was slow and expensive, with months spent on data and tuning and a lot of duplicated infrastructure and evaluation effort across projects that hurt efficiency and ROI.

Chao sees this era as maximizing model accuracy via custom pipelines but paying the price in iteration speed, shared tooling, and the ability to move quickly on new ideas.

Blurry roles

Historically, data scientists chased model accuracy while engineers owned system stability, latency, and throughput, but with strong base models the line is blurring and both roles now focus on shipping useful products with overlapping responsibilities.

Engineers still push on making systems stable, while applied scientists explore the boundaries of base models and orchestrate them into new features, so the difference between the roles is more about emphasis than fundamentally different job descriptions.

Problem definition

Chao argues that in the large‑model era, the biggest ROI comes from precise problem definition that does not limit itself to what current models already do but imagines what products would be genuinely useful in real life.

Evaluation loops

He emphasizes robust evaluation and tuning mechanisms, including automated feedback loops and telemetry that help systems adapt and better meet customer needs over time.

Cross‑domain

Chao believes the ability to combine domains to define complex problems and design validation and automatic evaluation now delivers higher ROI than narrow single‑domain depth alone.

Prompt for the 90%

Chao’s prompt‑tuning approach is to first define a general prompt that works for roughly 90% of typical cases, then systematically collect edge cases and failures where it behaves badly and augment the prompt with explicit constraints to handle that last 10%.

He stresses that success means fixing those bad cases, not just making demos look good, and that prompt tuning alone is not a silver bullet for hallucinations or error accumulation in complex multi‑step systems.

Planner vs Executor

To fight error accumulation, Chao proposes a planner–executor architecture where a planner, often an LLM, does high‑level reasoning and outputs structured steps like “move forward three steps,” while an executor component maintains authoritative state, verifies each action, and feeds failures back so the planner can retry instead of letting small inaccuracies snowball into garbage outputs.

From an ROI perspective, Chao highlights that five years ago deep single‑domain expertise dominated, but in the current LLM era the most valuable skills are combining domains to define complex problems, designing validation and automatic evaluation, and integrating telemetry and adaptive loops to keep systems aligned with real customer needs.

Ramesh emphasizes that a successful career means evolving with the industry, being willing to learn new things instead of staying comfortable with what you already know, especially through big shifts like the current AI transition.

He compares today’s AI wave to the disappearance of dedicated manual tester roles, where testers who embraced automation and engineering transformed into software engineers empowered by tools.

The host echoes that testers who adopted automation could evolve, and Ramesh argues that in the same way, engineers now need to evolve to leverage AI tools and workflows to stay relevant.

Chao adds a bit of humor by joking that one way to avoid being replaced by AI is to retire before it takes your job, underlining that change is inevitable and adaptation beats denial.

Chao’s key advice to juniors is to “make every step solid,” meaning your code should work as well as it can and your designs should be good enough before you move on, and that a question–answer pair only counts if you push until you truly understand the answer; combined with staying curious about AI trends, using task‑driven tools, and practicing complex problem definition, this mindset lets you grow with each paradigm shift instead of being left behind.
